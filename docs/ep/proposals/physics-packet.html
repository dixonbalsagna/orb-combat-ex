Fights by physics: the packet

From your stand-in, 2026-11-02. It builds on the EP's first sketch (docs/ep/proposals/physics-model.html) and adds an audit of every system that moves a fighter or touches the world. It's design only: nothing is built, and every number is a first value. The diagrams below are toys that use the model's formulas, not the game.

In one minute. Every hit has a damage number and a force number. Force over the target's weight gives a speed, and that speed decides everything else: a stagger, a knock-back, a launch, a burning trail, a meteor, and how big the crater, trench or skip is. Your two named features fit in cleanly. Skims become a body spending its force on each skip. Trenches become the force left at touch-down, spent against the ground's grip. The audit also turned up 15 things worth knowing. The biggest is that a hidden sideways speed boost of up to 6 times is behind much of the "too fast to read" problem, and it also breaks the skim rule. And in a small set of new ideas, the world takes the force a fight puts into it and hands it back.

1. The model, revised

The first sketch, with your answers from today folded in. Six rules:

1. Weight works both ways

Everyone starts at 100 and gets heavier as they transform. A heavier body is harder to move and hits harder, so two equally transformed fighters cancel out. Heavier is slower to stop too: it takes longer to recover in the air and to brake out of a slide.

2. The fight's shape stays, the scale grows

Whether a hit staggers, knocks back or launches is judged against the defender's own power. Two maxed fighters brawl with the same rhythm as two at base. The burning trail and the meteor are fixed speeds, because the sky is the same for everyone.

3. Force hurts where it can't move you

Base damage is per attack. Extra damage comes only where force can't become movement: slamming into the ground or a building, being pinned, or blocking. It's not the fall that hurts, it's the sudden stop.

4. You can see the force

Your charge stages, 2 at base and up to 6 at max, are steps of force on the fighter's body. A player knows roughly what a release will do before letting go. That's the cure for "knockbacks feel arbitrary".

5. A launch knows its whole flight

At the hit, the game works out the path, the landing, the crater and the buildings crossed, and hands that plan to the camera, the choreographer, the effects and the world. The choreographer can bend the aim but never add force.

6. Each planet, its own gravity

A big throw should feel big on every world size, from tiny to COLOSSAL. All of it is the game's own maths, never Godot's physics engine, so both PCs on LAN always agree.

attacker's force   = power energy × the attack's share × (attacker weight ÷ 100)
speed of the hit   = 900 × √(force × 100 ÷ defender weight)
outcome            = force ÷ the defender's own power   →  stagger · knock-back · launch
burn and meteor    = by speed alone: glow from 4,500, burning from 6,000, meteor past the top line
crater             = radius 160 × √(force at impact)
damage             = the attack's own number + what was lost in a sudden stop

3. The pebble skip

You asked: "I don't want to lose the 'pebble skip' we have when fighters get knocked across the surface of a body of water". Today a launched body skims if it's going over 500 and comes in shallower than about 31°, up to 6 times. Each skim keeps 85% of its speed. (sim/world/contact.gd:566-599, data/biomes/contact.json)

With physics each skip spends a slice of the body's force, roughly a quarter of it, and that slice goes into the water as spray. A skip needs speed over a sink line that rises with weight and gravity, so a heavy transformed body skips fewer times and flatter, and a light one skips more and higher. Ice is the same kind of surface with a break-through threshold.

Found: the skim test reads the on-screen sideways speed, which the hidden boost multiplies by up to 6, so nearly every sideways send counts as "shallow". Encounter's parked bounce fix would also cut skims from 6 to 3, because skims and ground bounces share one counter. My recommendation: skims get their own count, decided by force, and the boost goes.

4. The trench

You asked: "dragging their feet or hands along the ground to stay upright, which leaves a deep trench, shatters concrete, and kicks up massive clouds of dust". Your friend's words: "Love when they carve a trench". Today a slide brakes at a fixed rate times the ground's grip, plus a drag that grows with speed. The trench's width grows with the square root of the energy, and its depth with speed, capped at half a fighter height. (sim/world/slide.gd, contact.gd:1075-1089)

With physics the force left at touch-down sets the trench's length, width and depth. The braking law the code already has stays, because it's good. The sketch's guess of "a quarter of the carry" can go. Each ground gets two numbers, hardness and grip:

Found:
  • At base, a knock-back never leaves a trench at all. Anything under 4 body heights is carried upright by the director, against your "a small crater or trench" at base.
  • The trench is capped at 152 deep, about 2 body heights, while craters go much deeper, so "longer and wider trenches" at high power hit a ceiling.
  • The trench's power term counts power twice once force carries it.
All three are small fixes once force decides the trench.

5. Readable at any speed

You like the speed, but the acceleration and sheer speed can make fights hard to read. The audit found why fighters get lost or tiny. Bodies cross the screen faster than the eye can follow, past about 1.9 screen widths a second (Camera's own research). When the camera zooms out to keep up, it shrinks fighters to 23 to 41 pixels. And the camera reacts after a body has moved, so it whips.

The fix is a published flight plan. The game knows the whole flight at the hit, so the camera can start easing out and frame the landing during the held moment of the hit, before the body moves. It leads instead of chasing. Below, the same launch is shown both ways:

Left: today, the camera reacts. Right: the plan is known at the hit, so the camera is already there.
The readability budget, which every launch is checked against:
  • Speed cap on screen: a fighter never crosses the screen faster than the eye can follow. The camera eases out ahead of a fast launch to stay under it.
  • Always see yourself: your own fighter is never out of your picture.
  • Size floor: fighters never shrink to specks. Over empty sea or sky the wide shot doesn't open, and the trail carries the read.
  • A held moment that grows with force: a short pause on the hit, longer for a bigger send. It gives the eye the start of the flight, and the camera its head start. Camera has asked for 14 to 18 ticks on a launch.
  • Remove the hidden sideways boost: on-screen speeds fall from a median of about 10,800 to at most 14,000 at max and 4,000 at base. That alone takes most launches back inside what the camera can follow.

6. What the audit found

Four read-only reviewers went through the code, the docs and all of your vision, with a skeptic checking every claim against the files. These are the findings I think you'd most want to know, in plain words.

  1. A hidden sideways boost of up to 6 times is applied to every launch, and 10 times to free flight far from the rival. Damage, craters and braking read the real speed, but the screen shows the boosted one. It's the single biggest cause of "too fast to read", and it breaks the skim test. constants.gd:17
  2. More power means more bounces today, 1, 2, 3 and 3 by tier, the opposite of what you asked. A tumble's clock also restarts at each landing, so a rattle can run to the limit of 8 contacts, about the 6 or 7 you saw. contact.json:21, contact.gd:747
  3. On main, the villain still gains power from civilian deaths (menace and power, every casualty he causes). That breaks pillar 5 as you approved it on October 31. It's known and queued, but not yet built. sim/world/collateral.gd:351-356
  4. Base and max leave the same crater, because ordinary blows are capped at a radius of 600. crater.gd
  5. A big clash is a dice roll weighted by tier, not a contest of force. templates.json 698-710
  6. A guarded beam launches the blocker, at a fixed force, which contradicts your picture of a catch. beam.gd 159
  7. Most beam struggles leave no mark, while you pictured the biggest crater at the meeting point. The fix is designed and Legal has read it (RL-246).
  8. The rule meant to make upper floors crush lower ones never fires, because it compares a count of floors with hit points. brunt.gd:1041
  9. There are six separate flight predictors. The one the launch planner uses differs most from the real flight, so a chosen landing often isn't where they land. The camera's launch cue is built on it too. One flight plan fixes all of it.
  10. Gravity is written into at least ten places, and tripled after a body's first contact as a tuning shortcut. Per-planet gravity needs one number.
  11. There's no air recovery on main. The mid-air brake and its skid pose were built weeks ago and are switched off, so you've never had them in a build.
  12. Repeat launches break less, not more. A rule meant to stop repetition is squared into the energy, against your "the more destroyed it will be". It's off on main today.
  13. Several things have no weight yet. Thrown wrecks, cars and fighters use three mass scales that don't agree.
  14. An old camera paper for the space fight uses dissolves and cross-fades, which you rejected. It has to be rewritten before any of it is built. exosphere.md
  15. The sketch's own table breaks three of your rulings. It has a flick knocking back at tier 3 (you ruled the stick never sends), a tapped B launching at tier 3 (you ruled a tap staggers), and lights knocking back at max. Rule 2 above fixes all three.

9. Your calls

Things only you can settle, smallest first:

  1. A big hit on a guard: should the blocker slide back and carve a trench? Your melee prototype said "a blocker is never knocked back". I think the slide is a great moment, and it fits the beam catch you asked for. It's your rule to change.
  2. An even mash: pure physics would cancel two equal hits to nothing, but you asked for "both thrown back, nobody wins". I'd keep yours: both are shoved back, and the cancelled force goes into the ground as a crater under them.
  3. The meteor: one ordinary launch past the top line, as you asked, rather than Game Design's separate authored curve. Today's curve does complete the arc. I'd keep its shape only as a fallback.
  4. Gravity by world size: do smaller worlds get stronger gravity so a big throw covers a similar share of the planet?
  5. Names: weight and force, from section 6 of my last note: heft or ballast, and clout.

When you say go: the order I'd suggest

  1. Land the parked energy model, Simulation's power core and World's power world, behind one switch.
  2. One flight plan, replacing the six predictors, published at the hit.
  3. Remove the hidden sideways boost.
  4. Weight, the two kinds of threshold, and damage at the sudden stop.
  5. Skims, trenches, bounces and craters spending force.
  6. The camera's readability budget, checked by clips: for each row of the sketch's table, can you say what happened and where everyone ended up?
  7. The new ideas, one at a time, each behind its own switch.
